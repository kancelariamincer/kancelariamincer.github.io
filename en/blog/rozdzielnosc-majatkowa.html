Separation of property: options, procedures and legal requirements

Separation of property is a legal mechanism that allows spouses to keep their personal assets separate. It can be used before marriage, during the marriage and, exceptionally, with retroactive effect if there are important reasons.

1. Separation of property before the wedding (prenuptial agreement)

A marital property agreement, commonly called a prenup (intercyza), can be concluded before the wedding before a notary. Signing it means that after the marriage each spouse’s assets remain separate: no statutory joint property arises, and earnings and acquired assets become each spouse’s personal property. It is worth knowing, however, that a prenuptial agreement signed before the wedding takes legal effect only at the moment the marriage is concluded.

2. Separation of property during the marriage

Spouses may conclude an agreement on separation of property at any time during the marriage – this requires the consent of both parties and a notarial deed. From the moment such an agreement is signed, joint property ceases to exist and each spouse manages their own assets independently. If one party does not agree, the matter can be taken to court, which may order separation of property in court proceedings – so-called compulsory separation.

3. Separation of property with retroactive effect

Separation of property may also be established with effect from a date earlier than the date of the judgment if the court finds that important reasons arose earlier. Examples of such reasons include a long separation, lack of cooperation in property matters and no shared household. Retroactive separation can only be ordered by a court – the parties cannot arrange it before a notary. The court examines the circumstances of each case and states the specific date of separation in the judgment.

Note: retroactive separation cannot take effect earlier than the day on which the “important reasons” justifying it arose.

4. Statutory requirements and court procedure

The legal basis for a claim to establish separation of property is Article 52 of the Family and Guardianship Code. In court you must show “important reasons”, such as gross mismanagement, addiction, squandering of assets or not living together. The court hears the case in civil proceedings.

Required documents and course of the case:

  • a statement of claim to establish separation of property,
  • a justification setting out the important reasons,
  • a court fee,
  • a marriage certificate,
  • where applicable, documents confirming the circumstances.

After the proceedings, the court issues a judgment stating the date on which separation of property is established. The judgment is the basis for a possible division of property in separate proceedings.

5. Summary

Spouses’ assets can be separated at several stages: before the wedding (prenuptial agreement), during the marriage with the consent of both parties and – in special cases – retroactively on the basis of a court judgment. Each of these routes has its own formal requirements and legal effects.

The most important legal effects of separation of property:

  • End of joint property: from the moment separation is established, the spouses’ joint property ceases to exist. All future income, salary and acquired items or real property will be each spouse’s personal property.
  • Financial independence: each spouse manages their own personal assets and is responsible for their own financial actions. This means independence in making financial decisions, taking on obligations and investing.
  • Protection against the other spouse’s debts: a creditor of one spouse cannot enforce against assets belonging solely to the other spouse once separation of property has been established.
  • Individual liability for obligations: each spouse is liable for their debts only with their own personal assets. The exception is obligations incurred jointly.
  • No new joint property: after separation is established, no property common to both spouses arises. All future acquisitions belong to personal property.
  • Marital duties: separation of property does not remove other marital duties, such as mutual help, loyalty or contributing to the family’s maintenance.
  • No joint income tax settlement: spouses with separate property can no longer use the preferential joint tax settlement.
  • Inheritance: separation of property does not affect the rules of statutory succession.
